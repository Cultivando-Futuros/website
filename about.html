About the program

Real science, close to home

We are not accepting applications yet. We expect applications to open in February 2027. Notify me when the application opens →

1. Why we started this

Young people in farmworker communities are curious and smart. But many do not get the chance to do real science outside of class. We want to change that.

We also believe that people who live with a problem should help study it. Who knows a community better than the people who live there? Cultivando Futuros brings together high school students from the Salinas and Pajaro Valleys, college and graduate students, scientists, and community leaders to study health and environment questions that matter to local families.

2. Four research teams

Each team has 3 or 4 high school students, 2 college students from UC Santa Cruz, a graduate research consultant from UC Berkeley, and several adult mentors, like scientists, health workers, and community leaders.

Epidemiology

Study health patterns in our community. Who gets sick, and why?

Environmental sciences

Study water, air, soil, or pesticides.

Medical science

Learn about health care and the health problems farmworker families face. Team members may get to shadow health care workers at Clínica de Salud del Valle de Salinas.

Science education

Make science learning tools for schools and the community, or study how science is taught.

3. Everyone learns. Everyone teaches.

We call this "mentoring the mentor." Everyone on a team learns from someone and helps someone else learn.

  • Scientists and community leaders guide the teams and mentor the college and graduate students.
  • UC Berkeley graduate students give the teams research advice as research consultants.
  • UC Santa Cruz undergraduates mentor the high school students and work side by side with them.
  • High school students lead their team's project and help each other grow.

4. Your year, step by step

  1. SpringThe whole group meets. Every two weeks, guest speakers like farmers, health workers, community advocates, and scientists talk about their work and how they got their jobs.
  2. Late springEveryone joins a team, and each team picks a question to study.
  3. SummerTeams do their research. Hands-on workshops teach new skills. (Workshops continue into the fall.)
  4. Fall and winterTeams look at their results and figure out what they mean.
  5. End of the programTeams share what they found with the community and local leaders.

5. What teams make and share

  • Each team writes a short, two-page plan for its project. The program pays for supplies, like testing kits.
  • Hands-on workshops cover research ethics, how to write a survey, basic statistics, and how scientists measure pollution.
  • Before presenting to the public, each team practices in front of all the mentors and gets helpful feedback.
  • Teams share their findings with families, schools, local leaders, and the county agricultural commissioner.
  • Each team makes a real product, like a poster, a short paper, a podcast, or a teaching tool. Team members are named as authors.
  • Students keep an online journal (ePortfolio) about their work and ideas.

6. Where it happens

Most activities take place at the CHAMACOS office on the Natividad campus in Salinas, at local schools, or at places in the community. To apply, you must speak and understand both English and Spanish. We use both languages in all activities.

7. Two groups of students

We will run the program two times.

  • Group 1: We expect applications to open in February 2027 and the program to start in March 2027.
  • Group 2: about a year after Group 1

If we have more applicants than spots, we will create a waitlist for the second year of the program.

8. Built on trust

Cultivando Futuros brings together partners that farmworker families in the Salinas and Pajaro Valleys already know. They have worked together for years, including on a county agriculture coalition during the COVID-19 pandemic.

  • Clínica de Salud del Valle de Salinas has cared for farmworker families for decades. It grew from a volunteer-staffed clinic into a community health center with 14 clinics serving more than 60,000 patients a year.
  • Center for Community Advocacy is rooted in farmworker families. Since 2014, its Youth for Change program has helped Salinas youth get involved in their community and plan for college.
  • The CHAMACOS study at UC Berkeley has worked with Salinas Valley families for more than 25 years. It has produced more than 200 scientific papers and helped shape pesticide rules.
  • CSU Monterey Bay: Dr. Corin Slown led an environmental science and art program with Dr. Brenda Eskenazi that introduced more than 4,000 students to environmental science. She has working relationships with five Monterey County school districts.
  • UC Santa Cruz: Dr. Matt Sparke of the Global and Community Health program works with the Center for Community Advocacy through the Campo-Sano collaboration, and the program's internships train UCSC students in community-engaged research.

Earlier youth projects by these partners showed what young people can do. Young people co-wrote scientific papers and helped create a public mural in Salinas that shows research findings.

Cultivando Futuros is a partnership of UC Berkeley, UC Santa Cruz, CSU Monterey Bay, Clínica de Salud del Valle de Salinas, and the Center for Community Advocacy.
